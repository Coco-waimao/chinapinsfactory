Process

Five processes, judged on the same criteria

Most suppliers show you their best process. This page puts all five side by side, including where each one is the wrong answer. The tables below are the same specification sheet we work from when we quote your order.

Why the process matters more than the finish

Two pins can carry the same logo, the same plating and the same Pantone references and still be entirely different products. One is a 0.3 mm brass sheet with enamel poured into it and ground flat. The other is 1.2 mm of solid brass struck into relief and left as bare metal. The first is flat, bright and inexpensive to produce. The second has depth, catches light on the edges and will not chip.

The process decides the metal, the colour count, the minimum line width, the tooling and the lead time. It is the first decision on the order and the hardest one to change later.

Each process below has its own full specification on the product page, including base metal, plating thickness, tooling life and the finishing options. The tables on this page compare them against each other.

Tooling, lead time and durability

This is the commercial half of the decision. Tooling is the part that gets paid for once and has to be amortised, so it matters most at small quantities and least at large ones.

Process Tooling Lead time Durability
Hard enamel CNC steel mould, cut in house, archived three years 7 to 10 business days Handles daily pocket use without scratching the enamel
Soft enamel CNC steel mould, cut in house, archived three years 7 to 10 business days Good. A thicker film resists chipping better than hard enamel
Die struck Steel stamping die on a hydraulic press to 200 tonnes 7 to 10 business days The most durable option, no enamel to chip or crack
3D die cast Two-part casting mould, the most complex tooling of the five 7 to 10 business days Cast metal with no enamel face, built for handling

How to choose in three questions

If the tables have not settled it, these three questions will. Answer them in order and the process usually falls out on its own.

Does the design have a gradient or a photograph in it?

Then it is offset printed, and the question is settled. No other process will hold a gradient, because enamel is a solid matched-to-Pantone material and it does not blend.

Does the design have moving parts, internal cutouts or real depth?

Then it is 3D die cast. Stamping cannot produce a hinge, a spinner or a part that rolls, and a two-part casting mould is the only way to make one.

Is the piece going to be handled hard, and does it need to be all metal?

Then it is die struck. Solid metal, up to 1.5 mm thick, with relief formed by a 200 tonne press. Nothing to chip, nothing to crack, and it will look the same in five years.

If the answer to all three is no, you are choosing between hard enamel and soft enamel. That decision comes down to colour count and gloss: up to four references with a crisp raised metal line, or up to six with a brighter, glossier fill.

Three mistakes that cost money

Specifying a process before specifying the artwork

A design that will not work in the chosen process is redone, and redone artwork means a new proof, a new approval and a new mould. Send the artwork first and let the process follow from it.

Assuming a thicker pin means a better pin

Thickness is a consequence of the process, not a quality upgrade you can buy. Die struck is thicker because the process requires it, not because it is better. A 0.3 mm hard enamel pin and a 1.2 mm die struck pin carrying the same design are different products with different prices, and neither is the wrong answer.

Forgetting that a reorder is a different order

We archive moulds for three years, so a repeat order within that window does not pay tooling again. But it does carry the specification you approved the first time, which means a change of artwork, a change of size or a change of plating is a new tool, not an adjustment.
