How to order custom lapel pins from China

What happens between your enquiry and the cartons arriving, what is negotiable, what is fixed by how the process works, and where new buyers most often get caught out.

China Pins Factory · 18 October 2026 · 9 min read

Sourcing custom lapel pins from China is straightforward once you understand that you are buying a manufactured product, not a catalogue item. The process runs in defined stages, each with its own timing, and the decisions you make early determine how long the whole thing takes.

This guide walks through the stages in order, from the first enquiry to the sealed cartons, and explains what is genuinely negotiable, what is fixed by how the process works, and where new buyers most often get caught out.

Before you send an enquiry: what a factory actually needs

The quality of the quotation depends entirely on how precisely the request is specified. A supplier who has to guess will either quote slowly or quote for the wrong thing. Six pieces of information remove all the guessing.

  • Artwork in a vector format, or a photograph if that is all you have
  • Quantity, and whether it is a one-off or a repeat programme
  • Size, in millimetres or inches, measured as the overall diameter
  • Finish, or at least a reference point such as a sample you like
  • Delivery date and destination country
  • Packaging, if you have a preference. If not, say so and we will advise

Notice that nothing here requires you to know the manufacturing process. That is our job, and you would be paying us for the privilege. If you are uncertain between hard enamel and soft enamel, describe the artwork and we will tell you which one holds it better.

Stage one: the 2D and 3D CAD proof

Within 12 hours of a complete enquiry you receive two things: a flat artwork proof showing exactly what will be cut, and a rendered 3D view of the finished pin in the finish you selected. Both are free and neither commits you to anything.

This stage matters more than it first appears, because almost every correction happens here. A logo that looked right as flat artwork may have its fine detail lost at 20 mm. A colour that looked correct on screen may sit too close to the metal line once the enamel is polished flush. The 3D render shows you the second problem before any steel is cut.

Our in-house designers also vectorise from whatever you send. A photograph of a physical badge or even a hand sketch is genuinely enough to start, and the redraw comes back to you as a proof for approval.

Stage two: reading the quotation properly

The quotation is itemised rather than a single figure, and it is worth knowing which lines are which. Tooling, production, plating, packaging and freight are separated so you can see where the money goes.

Two things on that sheet deserve attention. The first is the volume rate. There is no separate discount negotiation to run, so the rate applied is calculated from your quantity and shown to you plainly. The second is the tooling line. If you intend to reorder, ask whether the mould is archived, and for how long. Ours are kept for three years, which means a repeat order runs on the original tool.

We do not publish prices. A pin is specified by size, process, base metal, plating and colour count, and a single published number would be wrong for most orders. It also compresses every negotiation into the last decimal of a currency.

Stage three: tooling and the mould

Once the artwork is approved, the steel mould is cut in our own tooling shop. For stamped pins this is hardened SKD11 steel cut on one of our CNC engraving centres. For 3D pieces it is a two-part die casting mould, which is what allows internal cutouts and moving parts. Offset printed pins need no steel mould at all, only a print plate, which is why they carry our shortest lead time.

Tooling is the slowest part of the order and the part most likely to surprise a new buyer. One mould covers one design at one size. A second size means a second mould. If you are unsure whether you will want a second size, deciding before the mould is cut is much cheaper than deciding after.

Stage four: the sample, and why it is worth the wait

A physical sample is produced on the same tooling and to the same plating standard as your production run. It arrives in three to five business days, and the sample cost is credited against the production order if you proceed.

Hold it. Check the plating under a raking light, look at the enamel against the approved proof, test the clutch, and measure the diameter. Plating that looks even under a warehouse fluorescent tube can show unevenness in daylight. The sample is the last cheap moment to change anything, because after approval the run is committed.

Samples are available from a single piece. If you are evaluating several finishes or several suppliers, ask for a sample set rather than committing to a production quantity.

Stage five: production, and the difference between a date and a window

Standard production runs 7 to 10 business days after sample approval. That is a window, not a single day, and understanding the difference protects you from a bad promise.

A 5 business days rush is possible when a production window allows it, and we confirm that against our own capacity rather than offering it optimistically. Since we run the presses, the plating line and the inspection bench ourselves, a rush is a scheduling question we can answer. With a trading company it depends on a third factory that may already be committed.

Volume also affects timing. Larger runs are scheduled as dedicated production slots, which are reserved against your delivery date, while smaller runs share the current window with other work.

Stage six: inspection, and what is actually checked

Inspection happens at every handover between workshops, not just at the end. That matters because a defect caught at the plating stage can be reworked, while the same defect found after packing means the whole carton is opened.

The checkpoints are shape and relief after stamping, surface scratches after polishing, plating coverage and lead content after plating, fill level and colour match after enamelling, clutch fit after assembly, and a two-person count and seal before the cartons are closed.

Pieces that fail are reworked or remade. They are not separated into a cheaper carton and shipped quietly.

Stage seven: packing and the shipment itself

Packaging options run from a single polybag, through blister cards and header bags with your own print, to gift boxes and bulk carton packing for your own distribution. For distributor programmes we can blind drop-ship, so the cartons and the packing list carry your branding and no reference to us.

The bulk order guide covers each packaging option and how it affects the packing steps and the shipping cost.

Stage eight: choosing the right trade term

The trade term you choose decides who handles freight, insurance and customs. For a first order, DDP is often the least risky because the delivered cost is what you agree to, with no freight surprises arriving later.

Once you are shipping regularly, FOB usually gives you better control if you have your own freight forwarder. Sea freight becomes worth comparing once a shipment is large enough for the difference in transit time to matter less than the cost per piece.

Remember that the importer of record is your entity unless you have agreed a DDP arrangement. That means customs declaration, duty payment and destination-specific compliance requirements sit on your side of the border.

The mistakes that cost new buyers money

In roughly sixteen years of doing this, the same problems keep recurring. All of them are avoidable.

  • Skipping the sample to save a week. A sample mistake costs a production run. A sample week costs a week.
  • Approving artwork in a hurry. Detail lost in the proof stage is detail lost for good, because the mould encodes it.
  • Leaving packaging unspecified. The default is a polybag, which is rarely what a retailer needs. Say what you want.
  • Comparing quotations on the headline figure only. Check what is included in tooling, packaging and freight before comparing.
  • Treating the delivery date as a promise. Ask for the window and the commitment, in writing, on the quotation.
  • Forgetting the destination rules. Lead content and finish requirements differ by market. Confirm them with your broker before ordering.

Planning a repeat order

If you are running a programme rather than a one-off, the second order should be the easiest order you place. Our moulds are archived for three years, so a repeat runs on the original tool with no re-tooling stage and a production start rather than a mould-to-production start.

Tell us at the first order if a repeat is likely. We can then hold the enamel reference and the plating standard so the second batch lands on the same colour as the first. Our factory page describes how colour is held across a run and how repeat consistency is achieved.
