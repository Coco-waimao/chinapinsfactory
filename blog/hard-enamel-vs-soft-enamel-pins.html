Hard enamel vs soft enamel pins

Same metal, same plating, same artwork file. The difference is whether the enamel sits level with the surface or below it, and that changes colour depth, durability and what your design can carry.

China Pins Factory · 12 October 2026 · 7 min read

Hard enamel and soft enamel pins use the same base metals, the same plating line and essentially the same artwork file. The difference is a single question: does the enamel sit level with the metal surface, or below it?

That one decision changes the colour depth, the durability, the number of colours you can use and what your artwork is able to carry. Getting it right matters, because changing it after the mould is cut means new tooling.

What hard enamel actually is

In hard enamel production, the cavities in the metal are filled and the surface is then lapped flat on a stone until the enamel is level with the surrounding metal. The raised metal lines between the cavities therefore stand slightly proud of the colour field.

That flush result is the signature of hard enamel, and it is what gives the process its name. The enamel surface is flat and glossy, with no dome, and the metal line reads as a crisp, sharp border around each colour area.

Because the enamel is lapped back, it cannot hold a thick layer. Colour sits in a thin film, so hard enamel typically carries up to four Pantone references before the colours start interfering with each other.

What soft enamel actually is

In soft enamel production, the colour is poured into the cavities and left below the metal rim. Nothing is lapped back. The enamel keeps the depth of the cavity and naturally forms a slightly convex, glossy surface.

Because the enamel is never ground down, a thicker layer survives the process. That is why soft enamel looks brighter and more saturated, and why it can carry up to six Pantone references without the same crowding problem.

The visible trade-off is that the metal rim now stands above the colour, so the design reads with a raised border around every colour area. On some artwork that is exactly the effect you want.

Which one should you specify

Start with the artwork rather than with the finish. Ask what the design is doing.

If the design is a wordmark or a flat corporate logo, hard enamel is almost always the right answer. The lapped flush surface gives the crispest reading of fine detail, and the raised metal line does the work of drawing the shape without needing a heavy dark outline.

If the design is a character, a mascot or something with layered depth, soft enamel gives you more room. More colours fit without crowding, the brighter fill suits illustrative work, and the raised rim separates adjacent colour areas that would otherwise bleed into each other.

If you genuinely cannot decide, send the artwork and ask for both. A sample set across the two finishes is a low-cost way to settle it, and a sample comes from a single piece.

Three things people get wrong

Cloisonne is not soft enamel. Cloisonne uses a soldered wire outline to separate colours, which produces a distinctly different, more traditional Chinese cloisonne look with a glassier surface. Soft enamel uses the same mould as hard enamel with the enamel simply left recessed. The manufacturing route is different.

Enamel colour is not plating colour. A shiny gold pin and a brass pin can carry exactly the same enamel. Plating is the metal finish; enamel is the colour fill. They are specified separately, and the sample shows both together.

Glossy does not mean glass-covered. The gloss on hard enamel comes from lapping and polishing the enamel surface itself. A clear epoxy dome over artwork is a different process entirely, and that is what offset printed pins use.

How each one wears

Both are durable in normal use. Hard enamel is the harder-wearing of the two in day to day pocket use, because the thin flush film has almost no edge to chip from and the exposed metal line takes the abrasion instead. Soft enamel sits slightly recessed, which protects it further from direct contact, and a deeper fill is harder to scratch through.

Neither will survive being deliberately scraped or bent. If a pin will live on a uniform, sit in a wash, or be handled roughly, tell us when you order, and we will specify a thicker base metal and a more protective plating cycle.

How to specify it on your order

Write the finish as a single instruction on the artwork: hard enamel, glossy, lapped flush, or soft enamel, recessed fill, glossy. Then list the Pantone references for each colour area.

If you are unsure which will hold your artwork better, say that in the enquiry instead of guessing. Our process pages set out the detail, and the bulk order guide explains how the choice affects the sample and the schedule.

Once the sample is in your hand, comparing it against the approved proof in daylight is the final check. If the colour reads too flat for the design, that is the moment to change the finish, not after production starts.
