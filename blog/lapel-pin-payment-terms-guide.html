Custom lapel pin payment terms, explained

The money moves on a schedule, and so does the goods. Here is how a pin order is actually paid for: what the deposit releases, when the balance falls due, which currency you are invoiced in, and how all of that sits alongside the trade term on the same quotation.

China Pins Factory · 18 November 2026 · 11 min read

Most suppliers send a bank account and put the terms on the back of an invoice. You find out at the worst possible moment, on the day the mould is about to be cut and you are asked for money you had budgeted to pay in three months.

That is not a complaint about any particular factory. It is what happens when payment terms are treated as boilerplate rather than as part of the order. The terms are not one number. They are a sequence: how many payments, what triggers each one, what you hold in your hands when you make it, and which currency it is paid in. Get that sequence wrong and the money leaves before the goods do.

This is what a sensible payment structure looks like on a custom pin order, what each node buys you, and how the payment line relates to the trade term printed next to it.

Two nodes, not one payment

A production order runs on two payments. The first is a deposit taken with the order. The second is a balance due once the goods have been inspected and before they are dispatched. Sample orders are the exception and are covered further down.

Node What triggers it What it releases What you can hold
Deposit With the order The mould cut and a production slot The tooling line on the quotation
Balance After inspection passes, before dispatch The goods, packed and ready to ship The final inspection report, or the shipping documents
Sample orders Settled in full before dispatch Nothing held back, because nothing is reserved The sample invoice and the courier tracking number

The line that matters most is the second one. The balance falls due while the goods are still ours, which means the inspection is still something you can act on. Our inspection guide sets out what those checks are. Here the only point is the ordering: money first would mean the inspection happens somewhere over three thousand kilometres away, and you would be arguing about a photograph of a pin.

What the deposit actually buys

The deposit is often described as a percentage. It is more useful to describe it as what it pays for, because that is what you are actually buying when the money goes out. It releases two things: the cutting of the mould and a production slot against your delivery date.

The mould is the reason this is not a generic up-front payment. When the tool is cut, the specification is fixed into it. The metal is part of the specification, so changing from brass to zinc alloy is not a line change on a purchase order, it is a different tool. The base metal page sets out why the metal sits at the top of the specification rather than at the bottom.

That is also what makes a repeat order cheaper in tooling terms. Moulds cut in our tooling shop are archived for three years, so a repeat order inside that window runs on the existing mould with no new tooling cost, as long as the specification has not moved. The bulk order guide shows how that changes the schedule as well as the cost.

Three things should be legible on the quotation before you pay anything:

  • The deposit amount, written as a line rather than as a condition at the bottom of an email.
  • The specification the deposit is attached to: size, process, base metal and colour count.
  • What happens to the deposit if the specification changes, which on our quotations is treated as a change of order rather than as a change of mind.

The balance, and the evidence behind it

There are two ways to settle the balance, and the choice is yours rather than the factory's. You can pay against the shipping documents, which is the standard arrangement for an order where the inspection has already passed on our side. Or, if you prefer to pay before the goods leave, you can pay against a copy of the final inspection report.

That report is not a summary. Every piece is inspected under daylight before it is packed, the inspector's name goes on the batch record, and the record travels with the order. It is the reason a claim about quality can be settled with evidence rather than with argument.

The currency question nobody asks early enough

We quote in USD for most enquiries, with EUR, GBP, AUD and CAD available on request. The part worth knowing is what happens afterwards: the invoice currency matches the quotation currency, so there is no conversion surprise at the end of the order.

That matters more than it sounds. If your company keeps its books in EUR and the quotation comes back in USD, somebody in your finance department is going to convert it, and the rate they get will not be the rate you budgeted with. Asking for the quotation in your own currency removes the conversion from your side of the transaction entirely, and it removes the accounting entry as well.

Payment itself is normally by bank transfer. For established accounts other arrangements can be agreed, but they are agreed in the quotation rather than discovered at the first invoice. Bank transfer also has one practical habit worth keeping: confirm who the money is being sent to, confirm that the currency on the wire matches the currency on the invoice, and confirm who is paying the transfer fee, because a fee deducted in transit leaves the received amount short.

Price and payment are two conversations

It is worth being clear about which part of the commercial discussion is settled automatically and which part is negotiable. Volume rates apply automatically: the rate applied to your order is calculated from the quantity you order, and the quotation states it plainly so you can see exactly which band it landed in. There is no separate discount negotiation to run.

Payment structure is the other half, and it is a different conversation. The price on a pin order is computed from the specification; the payment schedule is agreed between two businesses. So a complete quotation should carry these lines and not just a total:

Line What it tells you
Tooling Quoted separately, a one-off cost for a mould that is yours to keep and reuse
Production The quantity band the order landed in, applied without a separate negotiation
Plating and finishing Which of the twelve surface treatments the order is priced against
Packaging Whether the goods are packed for your own distribution or for retail
Freight Which term the freight sits under
Payment terms Currency, the deposit and balance nodes, and how the balance is triggered

The cost factors guide covers what each of the price lines depends on, and why the quote is itemised rather than published.

Samples and first orders

A sample does not run on the deposit and balance structure, because there is nothing to reserve. Sample orders are settled in full before dispatch, and the sample cost is credited against the production order when you proceed, so you are not paying twice for the same piece. Freight is quoted at cost, and you get the tracking number with the invoice.

Three things are worth getting right on a first order.

  • Do not accept a balance triggered after the goods have shipped on a first order. A first order is buying a supplier's record as much as it is buying pins.
  • Agree what the balance is triggered by while the quotation is being prepared, not when the balance invoice arrives. The trigger is easier to negotiate in advance than to change later.
  • If the schedule conflicts with your own accounting process, say so before the order is placed. Some companies can only book a payment when the goods arrive, and it is far easier to arrange a documentary balance in advance than to negotiate one at the point of dispatch.

Our samples and prototyping page sets out the four kinds of sample and what each one can and cannot tell you before you commit to a production run.

Payment terms are not trade terms

These two get confused more often than any other line on a quotation, so it is worth separating them cleanly. A trade term, in the sense the ICC Incoterms rules define, decides where the goods and the risk pass from one party to the other and who is responsible for export clearance, international freight, insurance and import duty. A payment term decides when the money moves and in which currency.

They are independent. A DDP quotation with a deposit and a balance is normal and unremarkable. An EXW quotation with the balance due before inspection is also possible, and is a poor arrangement, because you would be paying for goods you have not seen. The trade term tells you where the goods are; the payment term tells you what you have when you pay.

EXW deserves a specific mention because it is often assumed to be the simplest option. It is the opposite: EXW means you collect from our workshop door, which means the export clearance, the freight booking and the onward handling become your problem rather than ours. It is rarely the best choice for a first order. Our guide to FOB, CIF and DDP compares the four terms side by side.

So a complete quotation should show two independent lines: the trade term, and the payment schedule. If either one is missing, ask for it in writing before the order is placed, because both are considerably easier to agree in advance than to correct once the money is due.
